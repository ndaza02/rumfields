Oil Analysis: Your Window into Equipment Health

📅 October 12, 2025 ⏱️ 5 min read

Robert Taylor

Diagnostics Expert

Oil analysis is one of the most powerful predictive maintenance tools available, yet many organizations underutilize or misinterpret its results. When properly implemented, regular oil analysis can predict failures weeks or months in advance, optimize drain intervals, and save thousands in repair costs—all from a simple fluid sample.

Why Oil Analysis Matters

Lubricating oil serves as the lifeblood of machinery, circulating through components and carrying information about their condition. By analyzing the oil, we can detect:

  • Wear Patterns: Identify which components are wearing and how rapidly
  • Contamination: Detect water, fuel, coolant, or dirt ingression
  • Oil Degradation: Monitor oxidation, nitration, and additive depletion
  • Operational Issues: Identify overheating, fuel dilution, or coolant leaks

Key Tests and What They Reveal

Viscosity

Viscosity measures the oil's thickness and resistance to flow. It's the single most important property for maintaining proper lubrication.

What Changes Mean:

  • Increased Viscosity: Oxidation, contamination with heavier oil, soot accumulation, or coolant contamination
  • Decreased Viscosity: Fuel dilution, thermal breakdown, shear degradation, or contamination with lighter oil

Action Thresholds: ±10% change from new oil viscosity warrants investigation; ±20% typically requires oil change.

Wear Metals Analysis

Spectrometric analysis detects microscopic metal particles in oil, indicating component wear.

Key Metals and Their Sources:

  • Iron (Fe): Gears, cylinders, crankshafts, bearings—most common wear metal
  • Copper (Cu): Bearings, bushings, thrust washers, coolers
  • Aluminum (Al): Pistons, bearings, thrust washers
  • Lead (Pb): Bearing overlays, solder, leaded bronze bushings
  • Chromium (Cr): Piston rings, cylinder liners, stainless steel components
  • Tin (Sn): Bearing material, piston coatings
  • Nickel (Ni): Valves, shafts, high-strength alloys

Interpretation: Gradual increases are normal wear; sudden spikes indicate accelerated wear or component failure.

Particle Count

Measures the quantity and size distribution of particles in the oil, expressed as an ISO cleanliness code (e.g., 18/16/13).

Why It Matters: Particle contamination is the leading cause of hydraulic system failure. Even particles smaller than the clearances between components can cause wear through three-body abrasion.

Water Content

Detects water contamination, which can cause:

  • Accelerated oxidation and acid formation
  • Additive depletion
  • Rust and corrosion
  • Reduced film strength
  • Bacterial growth

Acceptable Levels: Most industrial oils should contain <200 ppm water; hydraulic systems <500 ppm; gear oils <1000 ppm.

Acid Number (TAN)

Total Acid Number measures acidic compounds formed through oxidation and contamination.

Significance: Rising TAN indicates oil oxidation and approaching end of life. A TAN increase of 2.0 mg KOH/g above new oil typically signals need for oil change.

Base Number (TBN)

Total Base Number measures alkaline reserve in engine oils, critical for neutralizing acidic combustion products.

Critical for: Diesel engines, especially those burning high-sulfur fuels. Oil should be changed when TBN drops to 50% of new oil value or reaches 2.0 mg KOH/g.

Oxidation and Nitration

FTIR (Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy) analysis detects oxidation and nitration products.

Causes:

  • High operating temperatures
  • Air entrainment
  • Contamination with combustion products
  • Extended drain intervals

Sampling Best Practices

Sample quality directly affects result accuracy. Follow these guidelines:

When to Sample

  • Routine Monitoring: Sample at consistent intervals (operating hours or calendar time)
  • Equipment Condition: Sample when equipment is at normal operating temperature
  • Before Oil Changes: Sample just before draining to assess remaining oil life
  • Problem Investigation: Sample immediately when issues arise

Sampling Techniques

  • Use clean, dedicated sampling equipment
  • Sample from mid-stream of circulating oil when possible
  • Avoid sampling from drain plugs or sumps (settled contaminants)
  • Fill sample bottles completely to minimize air exposure
  • Label samples clearly with equipment ID, date, hours, and oil added

Interpreting Results

Trend Analysis

Single samples provide limited value—trends reveal the story. Track key parameters over time to identify:

  • Normal vs. abnormal wear rates
  • Gradual vs. sudden changes
  • Seasonal variations
  • Impact of maintenance actions

Correlation

Look for relationships between parameters:

  • Rising iron with increasing viscosity suggests oxidation and wear
  • High copper with water contamination indicates corrosive wear
  • Elevated silicon with high particle counts suggests dirt ingression

Common Failure Modes

Bearing Failure

Indicators: Rapid increase in copper, lead, or tin; may see iron if bearing has failed completely.

Action: Schedule immediate inspection; bearing failure can cause catastrophic damage.

Coolant Contamination

Indicators: High water content, elevated sodium and potassium, milky appearance, increased viscosity.

Action: Identify and repair leak source; change oil if contamination exceeds limits.

Fuel Dilution

Indicators: Decreased viscosity, fuel odor, low flash point.

Action: Investigate injector leaks, incomplete combustion, or excessive idling.

Dirt Ingression

Indicators: High silicon, elevated particle counts, increased wear metals.

Action: Inspect seals, breathers, and filtration; improve contamination control.

Optimizing Drain Intervals

Oil analysis enables condition-based oil changes rather than arbitrary time intervals:

  • Extend drains when oil remains in good condition
  • Change early when degradation or contamination exceeds limits
  • Reduce waste and costs while maintaining protection
  • Document savings for ROI calculation

Building an Effective Program

Start Small

Begin with critical equipment where failure has highest impact. Expand as you gain experience and demonstrate value.

Establish Baselines

Sample new equipment with fresh oil to establish normal parameters for comparison.

Maintain Consistency

Use the same lab, sampling procedures, and intervals for meaningful trends.

Act on Results

Oil analysis only provides value when results drive action. Develop procedures for responding to abnormal findings.

Return on Investment

A comprehensive oil analysis program typically costs $20-50 per sample but can deliver:

  • 10-40% reduction in maintenance costs
  • 20-50% extension of oil drain intervals
  • Elimination of 60-80% of catastrophic failures
  • 15-25% increase in equipment availability

For a single prevented failure, the program often pays for itself many times over.

Conclusion

Oil analysis transforms maintenance from reactive firefighting to proactive management. By regularly monitoring lubricant condition and wear patterns, organizations gain early warning of developing problems, optimize maintenance schedules, and dramatically improve equipment reliability. The key is consistency—regular sampling, trend analysis, and prompt action on abnormal findings. When properly implemented, oil analysis becomes your most cost-effective tool for maximizing equipment life and minimizing unexpected downtime.
